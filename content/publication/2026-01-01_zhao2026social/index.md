title = "Social Inference Under Partial Knowledge: A Bayesian Account of Mentalizing"

abstract = "An everyday challenge in social learning is aggregating information from multiple informants, each of whom may have access to a different aspect of a problem. In such settings, either identifying and following the most knowledgeable informant or averaging all views can weaken complementary evidence and lead to suboptimal decisions. We propose a normative solution based on mentalizing, also known as Theory of Mind, to infer the hidden evidence behind judgments and aggregate the inferred evidence to optimally update beliefs. We investigate whether people perform these sophisticated Theory-of-Mind-based computations when aggregating social information when given the opportunity to leverage the complementarity of informants' partial knowledge. Across three preregistered experiments, we had participants integrate judgments from agents whom they knew to have partially overlapping but distinct perspectives on the same problem to make their own judgment. We designed scenarios to separate normative predictions from several more or less sophisticated heuristic accounts. We formalized three levels of social inference: (i) Level-2 inference, full mentalizing that reconstructs evidence beyond stated judgments; (ii) Level-1 inference, heuristic belief updating by assigning greater weight to the agent (or combinations of agents) expected to be more informative given their access to the problem; (iii) Level-0 inference, unweighted vote tallying. Participants' responses were best captured by the less resource-intensive Level-1 scheme, and only a minority were best fit by Level-2 mentalizing. We conclude that people often fail to leverage the full implications of social evidence but suggest that this is also often a resource-rational strategy given the generally high cost-to-benefit ratio of mentalizing inferences."
